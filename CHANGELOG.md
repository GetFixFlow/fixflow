# Changelog

All notable changes to FixFlow CMMS will be documented in this file.

The format is based on [Keep a Changelog](https://keepachangelog.com/en/1.0.0/),
and this project adheres to [Semantic Versioning](https://semver.org/spec/v2.0.0.html).

## [Unreleased]

- Documents on assets and warranty dates (roadmap m04). The asset forms had a "Warranty Expiry" field that the server threw away, and there was nowhere to keep a manual or a photo of the nameplate (the AI assistant looked for manuals in an attachment slot nothing could fill). Now the asset has a **Documents** tab: technicians and managers add photos, PDFs and Word files (10 MB, checked by their contents) with a note, see who added them, and remove their own (a manager removes any). The assistant reads the first two PDF manuals of the asset, and names them. **Warranty expires on** is saved (new column `assets.warranty_expires_on`, in the audit log), and the asset page says how long is left, in amber within 30 days. A new daily job (8:30) sends managers and administrators one email, and a chat message if ticked, listing warranties that end within 30 days; each end date is sent once, and a changed date is sent again. New notification: "A warranty ends within 30 days" (Settings, Notifications and Team Chat). Work order attachments and asset documents now share one upload check (`UploadableFile`). Deleting a sample asset that has a document added to it is refused, like one with real work orders.
- Purchase requests and orders for parts (roadmap m03). When a part ran low, the only thing FixFlow did was send an email: nobody could ask for more, approve it, record the order or the delivery, and the stock was typed in by hand afterwards. A new **Purchasing** page takes a request through *requested, approved, ordered, partly received, received* (or turned down or cancelled). A technician asks for parts (the **Order** button on the Parts page starts a request with that part and enough to get back above its reorder point) and sees their own requests; a manager sees all, approves or turns down with a reason, places the order with a vendor (date expected, the vendor's own number), and records deliveries, which **add to the stock** under the part's row lock, in parts if it arrives in parts. Managers are told of a new request by email, push or chat (a new event, *Someone asks for parts to be bought*, in Settings, Notifications and Team Chat). Each company numbers its own orders PO-000001. A part or vendor on an order cannot be deleted; sample-data removal keeps such a part. New tables `purchase_orders` and `purchase_order_lines`. Not built: a document to send to the vendor, invoice matching, approval limits, a second approver, a change to the part's cost on delivery.
- Vendors and contractors (roadmap m02). There was nowhere to record the outside companies that do maintenance work, so their cost never reached the reports. A new **Vendors** page lists them (name, what they do, phone, email, website, address, notes), with the people to call at each; managers keep the list, technicians can read it, a vendor that has work orders is switched off rather than deleted. A manager can **give a work order to a vendor** and enter what they charged and their invoice number; the assignee stays. What a vendor charged counts in the work order's total and in **Cost Analysis** (a Vendor Costs figure, a series in the trend, a column per asset and place, and a Costs by Vendor table), and in the CSV exports. The charge and invoice number are sent to managers and administrators only, as wages are. Added: `GET/POST /vendors`, `GET/PATCH/DELETE /vendors/:id`, `POST /vendors/:id/contacts`, `PATCH/DELETE /vendors/:id/contacts/:id`, `GET /reports/costs/by_vendor`, `vendor_id` as a work order filter; new tables `vendors` and `vendor_contacts`, and `vendor_id`, `vendor_cost` and `vendor_reference` on work orders. The work order CSV gained `vendor` and `vendor_cost` columns, and the by-asset and by-place cost CSVs a `vendor_cost` column, so a script that reads them by position needs a look. Not done: contracts, insurance papers, rates, ratings, a vendor signing in, purchase orders (m03). Nothing to do when upgrading; the migration runs at start.
- Meters and maintenance by usage (roadmap m01). Choosing "meter-based" for a preventive maintenance schedule never did anything: it was saved, and nothing ever made a work order from it (its unit list even offered "km", which the server refused). An asset now has **Meters** (running hours, kilometres, cycles): a manager adds one at the reading the machine stands at, and a technician or manager records readings, which only count up (a lower one is refused; a manager can mark a replaced meter as a reset). A schedule can follow a meter ("every 250 hours"): when the reading reaches the due reading a work order is made, one at a time, and the next due reading follows the fixed series. A meter can be fed by a sensor that reports a running total. Added: `GET/POST /assets/:id/meters`, `PATCH/DELETE /meters/:id`, `GET/POST /meters/:id/readings`; new tables `meters` and `meter_readings`, and `meter_id` and `next_due_reading` on schedules. **Also fixed, found on the way:** the schedule form (create and edit) sent its template, checklist and parts under names the server never read, so a schedule made on screen had no work order template (the server refused it) and its checklist was lost; it now sends and loads the `template`. Not done: turning on/off signals into hours, unit conversion, backdated readings. Nothing to do when upgrading; the migration runs at start.
- Published images (roadmap d04). Every install built its two images on the customer's own server (5 to 10 minutes, and a build that can fail there). Each release now publishes `fixflow-api` and `fixflow-web` to GitHub's container registry (`ghcr.io/getfixflow`), for Intel/AMD and ARM, tagged with the exact version and never `latest`. The compose files name them after the release, and `make prod` is now `bin/up`: on a folder that is exactly a release it pulls them, so **nothing is built on the server**; on anything else, or if they cannot be pulled, it builds from the source as before. `make upgrade` and `make rollback` do the same. `FIXFLOW_BUILD=yes` always builds, `FIXFLOW_PULL=yes` always pulls, and `FIXFLOW_IMAGE_REPO` points at a mirror. `bin/release` keeps the compose files' version in step with `VERSION`, and CI's new `images` job (`bin/check_images`) builds the images, pushes them to a registry it starts, deletes them, and starts the stack from that registry only. **Not checked:** the release workflow's push to ghcr.io and the ARM build (they only run on a tag: tag a candidate such as `0.2.0-rc.1` to see them), and that a first release's packages are public, which a maintainer has to do once. Nothing to do when upgrading; `make prod` now waits until every service is healthy before it returns.

The scanner page existed but nothing in the app linked to it, and a scanned label sent the person to the asset list with the code in the address, which the list ignored, so it showed every asset. The top bar now has a **scan** button (not for requesters), and the asset list starts with the search in its address.
- A guide for technicians, a guide for administrators, and a Help link in the app (roadmap d19, text half). There was no page that walked a technician through a day, or an administrator through the first week. **Quick start for technicians** covers the home screen, finding your jobs, starting, holding and completing one, the checklist, photos, working with no signal and what a rejection does. **Administrator guide, first week** goes in the order that works: set up the company, backups, import and QR labels, people, scheduled maintenance, notifications. Both name the real screens and buttons. The person menu at the top right of the app has **Help and guides**, which opens the docs site in a new tab. The work-order status diagram was missing *assigned* and *on hold* and is corrected, and two pages said **Settings, Company**, which is **Settings, Organization**. Not done yet: screenshots.
- Email settings work (Day 9). Settings, Email never worked: the page had no server behind it, and it showed an empty form even when something was saved. Administrators can now enter the mail server (with presets for Microsoft 365, Gmail and a network server), send themselves a test email through what is in the form without saving it, and see the reason in plain words when it fails (wrong password, unknown host, nothing listening, wrong encryption). Saved settings (password stored encrypted, never shown again) are used for every email, ahead of the `SMTP_*` environment settings, and take effect without a restart. Changes are written to the audit log. **Run `rails db:migrate`.**
- Messages in a Slack or Microsoft Teams channel (roadmap p12, second half; p12 is complete). An administrator opens **Settings, Team Chat**, pastes a webhook address, ticks the events and presses **Send a test message**; FixFlow then posts when someone reports a problem from a QR label, a work order is assigned, work needs verifying, a critical or high sensor alert is raised, or a part runs low or out, each with a link back. "Slack" also covers Mattermost and Rocket.Chat on your own network; Teams gets an Adaptive Card message for a Teams workflow webhook. The address is a secret: it is kept encrypted, never shown again (only its host), and never written to the audit log. What people typed (a work order's title can come from the public request form) is made harmless first, so a `<!channel>` or a hidden link in Slack is plain text. Messages are sent by the background worker, tried again three times if the chat service is down, limited to 20 a minute, and the reason a message failed is shown on the page. The server refuses its own address and link-local addresses (cloud metadata services), accepts addresses on your own network, and does not follow redirects. New: an `organizations.chat_settings` column (migrated at start), `GET/PATCH/DELETE /api/v1/chat_settings` and `POST /api/v1/chat_settings/test` (administrators only), and a Team chat section on the Notifications docs page. **Checked:** 20 deliberate breakages of the new specs (all caught), delivery against a faked chat service (success, 4xx, 5xx, timeouts, redirects), the settings page, 846 web tests and 1,834 backend specs in the affected areas. **Not checked:** a real Slack, Mattermost or Teams channel (the Teams message is the documented form but has never been tried against Teams; the page says so), and the page in a browser. Nothing to do when upgrading.
- Push notifications to phones and computers (roadmap p12, first half; Slack and Teams messages are the second half). Settings, Notifications has a switch for **this device**: allow notifications once, and a message appears even while FixFlow is closed when work is assigned to you, a request you made is finished, a scheduled work order is created for you, and (for managers) work needs verifying, a problem is reported from a QR label, or a part runs low; tapping it opens that page. Each of those events has a Push tick beside its Email one, there is a master Push switch, a person can have 10 devices, and **Send a test** says what happened. It is Web Push: each message is encrypted for one browser and relayed by the browser maker's push service, so **no account with anyone and nothing to set up**; the server signs with keys it derives from `SECRET_KEY_BASE` (changing that secret makes every device turn push on again), and the background worker sends them. It needs HTTPS (browsers allow push only there) and an outbound HTTPS connection to the browser makers' push services. An address a browser gives that points inside the network is refused, at sign-up and again at send. New: the `web-push` gem, a `push_subscriptions` table (migrated at start), `GET/POST/DELETE /api/v1/push_subscriptions`, `POST /api/v1/push_subscriptions/test`, a `push_enabled` and per-event `push` in the notification preferences, and a new Notifications page in these docs. **Checked:** the messages are decrypted by a stand-in browser in the specs (RFC 8291) and the server's signature is verified, 18 deliberate breakages (all caught), the browser side against a faked service worker, push manager and permission prompt, 836 web tests. **Not checked:** a real browser or phone receiving a push (no browser was available), a real push service (Google, Mozilla, Apple), and the build of the new gem inside the Docker image (CI does that). Nothing to do when upgrading.
- One command to set up a fresh server, and guides for three kinds of server (roadmap p04). `deploy/install.sh` runs as root on Ubuntu 22.04+ or Debian 12+: it installs Docker with Compose from Docker's own apt repository, downloads FixFlow (the newest release, or `--version`) into `/opt/fixflow`, writes `.env` with every secret made by `openssl` (readable by root only, never overwritten), builds and starts it, waits until every service is healthy and prints the address. `--domain NAME` turns on HTTPS (a free Let's Encrypt certificate, or `--https-mode own-certificate|internal`), `--url` uses an address of your own, `--lite` installs the five-service version, `--no-start` stops before the build. It checks the system and ports 80 and 443 first, and refuses to touch an existing install. New pages: a "One-command install" section in Install on a server, and step-by-step guides for DigitalOcean, AWS (with the IAM policy the backups need) and a server inside the company network. Specs run the script's `--env-only` mode and hold the `.env` it writes to the server's own start-up check and to every setting the compose files require; the docs spec checks that every option the pages name exists. **Checked:** the script in a fresh Ubuntu 24.04 system: Docker installed from the apt repository, the clone, the refusals, the `.env`, and once the whole run through to a started, answering lite install (against Docker Desktop's engine). **Not checked:** a real server from a cloud provider (and so the provider guides, written from their documentation), a real Let's Encrypt certificate, Debian, an Arm machine, a company proxy. Nothing to do when upgrading.
- A lite install for small servers (roadmap p03). The full install runs seven services, including a file-storage server and a sensor broker that a small team may not need. `COMPOSE_FILE=docker-compose.lite.yml` in `.env` starts five instead (web, api, worker, PostgreSQL, Redis): uploaded photos and documents are a folder on the server's disk (the `uploads` volume), the nightly backups are another folder (the `backups` volume) or, with `BACKUP_S3_*`, another S3 storage, and sensors send readings over HTTP. It is the same code: `FILE_STORAGE=disk` switches Active Storage to the Disk service, and the backup store, the copy of the uploaded files, the backup list and download, the restore, Data Retention and the start-up check all understand a folder. `bin/compose` (docker compose with the install's file), `make`, `bin/upgrade` and `bin/rollback` read `COMPOSE_FILE`, so they work for either install. Backups hold the uploaded files as `files/<key>` on both installs, so a backup of one is meant to restore on the other. `bin/check_lite` (a new CI job) starts it from a fresh clone, uploads a photo over HTTP, takes a backup, loses the photo and the data, restores, and checks the five containers use under 800 MiB at rest (400 to 430 MiB measured). **Not checked:** a busy day, a 2 GB server of the kind a provider sells (measured on Docker Desktop), a real move between the lite and the full install, and any real S3 storage for the lite backups. Nothing to do when upgrading: the full install is unchanged.
- A documentation site, first version (roadmap p08). The README had grown to 690 lines of everything at once. The docs are now Markdown pages in `docs/` (install, HTTPS, configuration, people and roles, sign-in and security, importing, upgrading, backups, monitoring, troubleshooting, work orders, sensors, reports, the API, development), built into a searchable website by `mkdocs.yml`; the README is a short overview that links to them. New: a troubleshooting page, an API page (signing in, answers, errors, limits, examples checked against a real server) and a list of all 184 endpoints written from the routes by `rails docs:api`. A `docs` CI job builds the site and fails on a broken link; specs fail when the endpoint list is not the routes or when a page names a rake task, `make` target or `bin/` script that does not exist. A release attaches the site to the release as a zip and publishes its version with mike on the `gh-pages` branch. **Corrections found while writing it:** the old README gave the sign-in address as `/auth/login` (it is `/auth/sign_in`), named a sensor field `asset_tag` that is not read (`asset_id` takes the id or the tag) and a page-size parameter that does nothing (pages are 25 records; only `page` works). Nothing to do when upgrading. Links that pointed at README sections now point at pages in `docs/`.
- Accounts lock after wrong passwords, and new passwords must be stronger (roadmap p05, second half; p05 is complete). Ten wrong passwords for one account now lock it for 30 minutes (Devise lockable, time-based): the right password is refused too while it is locked, with the reason in the answer, and it opens by itself or an administrator unlocks it (Settings, Users, Unlock account; `POST /api/v1/users/:id/unlock`; the people list marks **Locked** and carries `locked`). A right password sooner starts the count again; for a person with two-factor sign-in the count starts again once the second step has signed them in. Locking and unlocking are written to the audit log. This is on top of the per-address throttle, which a guesser using many addresses gets past. New columns `failed_attempts` and `locked_at` on `users`. A password someone chooses must now be 10 to 128 characters (was 8) and not easy to guess (`PasswordPolicy`): the most common passwords are refused, also with digits or symbols added or letters swapped for look-alikes, as are one character or one short stretch repeated, a run of digits, the name of the app, and anything built from the person's email or name (parts of four letters or more). Only a password being set is checked, so existing passwords keep working until changed. The sign-up, accept-invitation, reset and My Profile forms ask for 10 characters and say what is refused; the sign-in form still accepts 8, so people with older passwords can sign in. Changed: `rails fixflow:create_admin` no longer falls back to a default password (`changeme!`); it asks for one and applies the rules. The demo accounts and the unusable password an invitation starts with are exempt. The scripts that start a whole stack (`bin/check_production_stack`, `check_https`, `check_upgrade`) use a password that meets the rules. **Run `rails db:migrate` (two columns on `users`).** **Upgrade:** anything that creates accounts through the API or `fixflow:create_admin` with a short or common password (a provisioning script, say) now gets a 422; give it a password of 10 or more characters that is not on the usual lists.
- Two-factor sign-in with an authenticator app (roadmap p05, first half). Settings, Security was invented from top to bottom: a fake "Enable 2FA" button that did nothing ("backend required"), a made-up login history ("Sri Lanka", "Firefox on Windows" flagged suspicious) and a session timeout that saved nothing. Now a person can switch on a real second step: Settings, Security shows a QR code (drawn in the browser; the key is also shown to type), the first code from the app switches it on, and ten one-time recovery codes are shown once. Signing in then asks for the password and a code (or a recovery code): `POST /api/v1/auth/sign_in` answers with `{two_factor_required, challenge}` and starts no session, and the new `POST /api/v1/auth/two_factor` takes the challenge (signed, good for 5 minutes, cancelled by a password change) and the code and signs in as before. Turning it off and getting new recovery codes need the password and a code. A code works once (no replay) and one 30 seconds either side is accepted; five wrong codes in a row stop all tries for 15 minutes (in the database, on top of a per-address throttle) and are written to the audit log, as are switching it on and off, new codes, a recovery code used, and an administrator's reset. The secret is stored encrypted with a key derived from `SECRET_KEY_BASE`, the recovery codes only as HMAC digests, and neither is in the audit trail. Administrators can require it for the company (Advanced Settings, a new company setting `require_two_factor`): a person without it is sent to a set-up screen and every other API call answers 403 `TWO_FACTOR_SETUP_REQUIRED` until they have set it up. An administrator can reset a person's two-factor (Settings, Users; `POST /api/v1/users/:id/reset_two_factor`, which also signs them out everywhere), and `rails fixflow:reset_two_factor[email]` does it on the server for the one administrator who lost their phone. The users' answers carry `two_factor_enabled` and `two_factor_setup_required`. New gem `rotp`. Removed: the invented login history and session timeout (where you are signed in is in My Profile). README: "Two-factor sign-in". Not yet (second half of p05): account lockout after wrong passwords and stronger password rules. **Run `rails db:migrate` (six `otp_*` columns on `users`).** **Upgrade:** changing `SECRET_KEY_BASE` now also stops everyone's two-factor sign-in; keep it.
- HTTPS is built into the Docker install (roadmap d15). The front door spoke plain HTTP only, so sign-in tokens and passwords crossed the network readable, and phones refuse the camera (QR scanning) and the offline cache without HTTPS. `docker-compose.prod.yml` has an optional `https` service (Caddy 2.11.7, pinned) in front of the web service, switched on with `COMPOSE_PROFILES=https` in `.env` and three modes in `HTTPS_MODE`: `automatic` (a free Let's Encrypt certificate for `HTTPS_DOMAIN`, renewed by itself, kept in the `caddy_data` volume), `own-certificate` (`certs/fixflow.crt` and `certs/fixflow.key`, which git and the images never get) and `internal` (Caddy's own authority; devices must trust its root certificate). Plain HTTP on port 80 is redirected. The plain-HTTP door can be moved and closed with the new `HTTP_BIND` (next to `HTTP_PORT`); the HTTPS service takes ports 80 and 443. The start-up check refuses an HTTPS setup that cannot work: a bad domain or mode, an `APP_URL` that is not `https://<HTTPS_DOMAIN>`, `HTTP_PORT` left at 80 or 443; and warns when the plain-HTTP door is still open to the network. `bin/check_https` (run by a new CI job) starts the stack with a certificate it makes and checks that exactly that certificate is served, the web app, API, sign-in and a WebSocket work over HTTPS with HSTS, plain HTTP is redirected, the plain-HTTP door is bound to 127.0.0.1, and `internal` mode serves a certificate from Caddy's authority. A Let's Encrypt certificate for a real domain is the one thing not tried. README: "HTTPS". No migration. **Upgrade:** nothing changes for an install that does not turn HTTPS on; to turn it on follow README, "HTTPS" (people sign in again at the new address).
- Releases and a tested way to upgrade and roll back (roadmap p01). There was no version to upgrade to, and no way back if an upgrade went wrong. A `VERSION` file now holds the version the server reports (System Health and `GET /api/v1/health`; `APP_VERSION` in the environment still overrides it). `bin/release 0.2.0` prepares a release (a branch with `VERSION` changed and `[Unreleased]` moved into a dated section; `--dry-run` shows the notes), and pushing the tag `v0.2.0` runs a new release workflow that checks the tag is on `main` and equals `VERSION` and publishes the GitHub release with notes from `bin/release_notes`: how to upgrade, then a **Before you upgrade** list made from changelog bullets that end with `**Upgrade:** what to do`, then the changes. `make upgrade TO=v0.2.0` (`bin/upgrade`) checks the server runs and no tracked file was edited, dumps the database to `backup_before_upgrade_<time>.sql.gz` and checks the dump is complete, notes the current version in `.fixflow_upgrade`, then checks out the new version and rebuilds and starts it (migrations run at start); if it does not come up healthy it says how to go back. `make rollback CONFIRM=yes` (`bin/rollback`) switches back and replaces the database with that backup, because migrations cannot be undone one by one; what was entered since is lost, uploaded files are kept. `bin/check_upgrade` is the test of all this and CI runs it on every pull request: it starts the version the change is merged onto, puts data in it (a company, a user, a place, an uploaded file), upgrades with `bin/upgrade`, checks the data, the migrations and the reported version, rolls back, and checks the old version and its data are back. README: "Upgrading and rolling back"; CONTRIBUTING: "Making a release". No migration. **Upgrade:** a server that runs a version without `bin/upgrade` upgrades by hand this once: `make backup`, `git fetch --tags && git checkout <version> && make prod`.
- Backups now include the uploaded photos and documents, and can be kept on another storage (roadmap p02, second half). The nightly backup copied only the database, and kept it on the same storage server and disk as the data, so losing the server lost both. Now the same run copies each uploaded file the backup does not have yet to `files/` in the backup bucket (nothing is ever deleted from the copy), and stores the dump last, so a listed backup has its files. `BACKUP_S3_ENDPOINT`, `BACKUP_S3_ACCESS_KEY`, `BACKUP_S3_SECRET_KEY` (and `BACKUP_S3_BUCKET`, `BACKUP_S3_REGION`, `BACKUP_S3_PATH_STYLE`) keep the backups on another S3 storage with its own login; the server checks them at start-up and the Data Retention page warns while the backups share the data's storage. `rails "fixflow:restore[...]"` and `make restore` now copy the files back too (`FILES=no` skips it), `rails fixflow:restore_files` does only that, and `rails fixflow:backup` makes a backup now. `bin/check_production_stack` stores a file, backs up, loses the file, restores, and checks it is back.
- The production Docker stack starts and works (roadmap t02). `docker-compose.prod.yml` had never been started: it could not be combined with `docker-compose.yml` without leaking the database and Redis to the network, the image would not build, the file storage image could not be pulled, `web` and `nginx` both claimed port 80 and nothing served the web app, and the broker needed a password file that did not exist. It is now one complete file: a single front door (`web`: the web app, with `/api` and `/cable` passed on; plain HTTP, `HTTP_PORT`), the API, the worker, PostgreSQL 16, Redis, a Mosquitto broker with a login, and SeaweedFS as the S3 file storage (MinIO's image is gone from Docker Hub; the `MINIO_*` settings keep their names). Only the front door and the broker's port are published; every image is pinned to a version; `.env.example` is the install file with the required settings first; `make prod`, `prod-status`, `prod-logs`, `prod-stop`, `backup` and `restore` wrap it. `bin/check_production_stack` starts it from a fresh checkout (images, health, front door, WebSocket, first-run setup, System Health, a backup and a restore) and a new CI job runs it. Found by starting it, and fixed: **uploaded photos and the logo could not be opened in any browser** (their links redirected to the storage server's internal address; they are now served through the app); **live updates were refused on every plain-HTTP install** (Action Cable took every request to be https because of `assume_ssl`; `APP_URL` and `ALLOWED_ORIGINS` are now allowed origins); **every sensor reading was stored twice** (the web server and the worker both listened to the broker; now only the worker does, `MQTT_SUBSCRIBER` overrides); the README told sensors the wrong MQTT topic and payload and an `IOT_API_KEY` that nothing reads (HTTP sensors use a key from Settings, API Keys); `RAILS_LOG_TO_STDOUT`, `CORS_ORIGINS` and `RAILS_MASTER_KEY` were set and read by nothing; the development compose file could not start (no development gems in the image, no secrets) and now runs with no configuration. Not done: HTTPS (d15), published images (d04), the install-on-a-clean-machine walkthrough (d01).
- Backups can be listed, downloaded and restored, and a failed backup tells someone (roadmap p02, database half). Before, the nightly backup went to the storage bucket and nothing could list, download or restore it, and a failure was only written to the server log (the code meant to email administrators called a mailer that did not exist). Now: `rails fixflow:backups` lists them and `rails "fixflow:restore[latest]"` (or a name, or a file path) restores one into an empty database, refusing a database that has data unless `REPLACE=yes`, checking the file is a complete dump first and doing the whole restore in one transaction. The Data Retention page lists the backups, downloads one (administrators only, each download written to the audit log) and warns when the newest is more than 26 hours old or the storage does not answer. A backup that fails is tried 3 times, 15 minutes apart, and after the last try every active administrator is emailed and a `backup.failed` line is written to the audit log. Found and fixed on the way: **Sidekiq's scheduled-job poller crashed at start-up** with the locked `connection_pool` 3.0.2 (Sidekiq 7.3 needs 2.x), so no retry or delayed job ever ran; `connection_pool` is now kept below 3 and a test guards it. The app image's `pg_dump` is version 18 but the compose database is PostgreSQL 16, and a version-18 dump starts with a setting version 16 refuses, so a plain restore into the stack's own database failed; the restore skips that line. Backups are now made without owners and privileges, so they restore under any database user. `make backup` used the wrong database user (`postgres`; the production one is `fixflow`) and now writes a gzipped dump. Uploaded files are not part of a backup yet.
- Data Retention is real (roadmap p09, second half). The page was invented from top to bottom: fake storage figures ("5.1 GB / 10 GB"), six retention policies that were never saved or applied, a list of backups that never happened and buttons that did nothing. Now it shows the real size of the database, its biggest tables and the uploaded files, and sets two limits that the nightly jobs really apply: sensor readings with their hourly summaries (7 days to 10 years) and the audit log (for ever by default, or 1 to 7 years). Before you save a shorter limit it asks the server what would be deleted and says so; each cleanup that deletes something writes a line to the audit log. Two real bugs are fixed on the way.
